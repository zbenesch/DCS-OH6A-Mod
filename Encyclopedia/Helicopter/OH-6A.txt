The OH-6A Cayuse, famously known as the "Loach" (derived from the LOH acronym), is a compact, teardrop-shaped scout helicopter that pioneered the light observation role for the U.S. Army. Entering service in 1966 during the Vietnam War, the OH-6A set 23 world records for speed, distance, and climbing, proving itself as an incredibly robust and survivable airframe. Its unique "egg" shaped fuselage acts as a reinforced roll cage, which gained a legendary reputation for protecting crews during the frequent crash landings associated with low-level scouting. In Vietnam, it often operated in "Pink Teams," acting as the bait to find enemy positions while an AH-1 Cobra loitered above to provide heavy fire support. Note: The OH-6A features a four-bladed main rotor and a distinct Y-tail (later changed to a T-tail in civilian MD 500 models) which provided excellent low-speed control but was sensitive to high-power torque settings. However, the aircraft is limited by its lack of sophisticated sensors; original Loach pilots relied on "Mark 1 Eyeballs" and flying at treetop level to spot footprints or disturbed foliage, making the mission profile extremely dangerous compared to modern unmanned scouts.
